1 · Frame plan 5 × 4 node grid · 20 identical fittings · 0 cuts

A 10-ft stick of EMT gives you 10-ft bays. 40 × 30 is four bays by three, which means every single pole in this structure — roof and vertical alike — leaves the bundle at 10 ft and goes straight into a fitting. No scoring tool, no saw, no deburring, no offcuts, no 8-ft-versus-10-ft sorting in a dusty box. The trade is that the ceiling sits at 10 ft instead of the more common 8 ft.

Rule — do not skip this one

All 20 five-ways must be rotated the same way. Look closely at an F5-1: it is open on one axis and closed on the other. Roof poles entering the open sides touch each other; poles on the closed axis end up more than 1¼" apart. Mix the orientations and the grid comes out uneven, which you will discover when the tarps won't stretch square. The guide's memory trick: line them up so you can see the Man through the 5-way. Pick the axis before you assemble the first cube, then never think about it again.

No couplers in this build. The reference guide specs couplers because it assumes a 10 × 12 ft grid, where the 12-ft side is a 10-ft pole plus a 2-ft pole joined mid-span. We are on a pure 10-ft grid, so there is nothing to join. If someone hands you a bag of couplers, they are not for this structure.
1" is the floor, not a preference. Do not substitute 3/4" EMT — it is far too whippy for playa conditions and many cheap fitting kits are secretly sized for it. Everything here is 1" (1.163" OD) and the F5-1's 1¼" inside diameter is matched to it.

The stronger alternative, for the record: 1-3/8" chain-link fence top rail is dramatically stiffer than EMT — EMT is literally designed to be bent, and it will bend in a serious blow. Top rail now runs about $24 a stick against EMT's ~$23, so the old price argument for EMT is gone. Recommendation: stay on 1" EMT for 2026. Switching means re-specifying all 20 fittings and all 20 footers to 1-3/8" four weeks out from gate, and one fitting type at one verified price is worth more right now than the stiffness upgrade. Revisit it next year before the frame gets reordered.

2 · Anchoring + ratchet straps Decided — lag screws + climbing hangers

A 40 × 30 solid roof is a 1,200 sq ft sail. One tarp catching a 50 mph gust vertically generates roughly 770 lb of force, and BRC whiteouts hit 50 mph with no warning and can run eight to twelve hours. Anchoring is the entire difference between a structure and a projectile.

The load path — memorise it

Ratchet strap over the 5-way → down the leg → S-hook into a climbing hanger → held by a 3/8" × 14" lag screw → driven through the footer into the playa. That tension is the only thing holding this structure to the ground. The eyebolts hold nothing. They are hand-tight positioners that keep poles from falling out while you build, and that is all they are. Footers keep the leg from punching into the playa and from sliding sideways; they do not hold anything down.

The anchor kit, per pole

ComponentPer poleSpec
Footer11" foot pad (FP-1), galvanized. Sits under the leg. Keeps it from sinking and from sliding.
Lag screw13/8" dia × 14" long. The top 6–8" of playa is dust — you need 12"+ of bite. One per footer; extra screws add nothing. Source: Fastener Superstore #476110, steel/zinc, $1.95 ea in a 50-pc carton.
Climbing hanger110 mm hole — exactly right for a 3/8" (9.52 mm) screw. Goes on under the lag head, gives the S-hook something to grab.
Vertical ratchet strap115 ft × 1", ~1,500 lb break strength, S-hooks both ends. Loops over the 5-way, hooks the hanger, twisted 1–2× around the leg.
Driver spec. 9/16", 6-point, deep impact socket. Do not buy 12-point — it rounds the lag heads. A cheap impact driver will eventually sink a screw; a 1/2"-drive impact wrench will save hours across 34 of them. Bring spare batteries, and ideally a second wrench.

Diagonal ratchet straps — mandatory here

With a bare flat roof and nothing on the sides, the 20 vertical straps would be enough. We have a side panel (§4), and side panels catch wind and dump that horizontal load into the frame. In heavy wind the failure mode is poles popping straight out of the 5-way headers, so the fix is compressive: diagonals that pull the whole structure in on itself.

  • Run them internally, inside the footprint. External diagonals are a trip hazard and sit where people walk; internal ones also brace in the direction the frame actually wants to fail.
  • Starting spec: 8 straps. One diagonal across each of the four corner roof bays, plus four in the vertical planes of the two faces the wind hits (SW) and the wing face. Adjust on site once you see which sides the RVs already shelter.
  • Same strap as the verticals — 15 ft, 1", S-hooks. Buy them together.
Lag screws sit flush and pull cleanly on teardown — nothing protruding, no MOOP. But the climbing hangers and strap runs are still at ankle height. Flag every one of them and light the perimeter. See Burning Man's Securing Your Structure.

3 · Roof tarps + bungees 12 × 10'×10' · one size only

The roof is twelve 10 × 10 solid tarps, one per bay, all identical. This is the single biggest quality-of-life decision on the sheet. One size means any tarp fits any bay, a torn one gets swapped in two minutes, replacements are the cheapest and most available tarp on the market, and nobody is ever digging through a dusty box looking for the one odd-sized panel. That search is exactly what turns a two-hour job into a six-hour one.

The 4–6" shortfall is a feature, not a defect. A tarp sold as 10 × 10 is a cut size; the hem is folded and sewn, so it arrives at roughly 9'6" × 9'6". That is exactly what you want on a 10-ft grid — the gap is what the bungees stretch across, and the tension is what keeps the panel from flapping itself to death.

Solid on top, mesh on the sides — never the reverse

Roof tarps must be solid. 100% solid casts materially better shade than 80% mesh, and there is no ventilation argument up there — playa wind is horizontal, it does not need to pass through the roof. Anything not horizontal must be mesh. A solid tarp used as a wall is a sail, and it will put destructive load into the frame. This is why the east wing is aluminet (§4) and not a poly tarp.

Colour

Spec is silver or light. Worth knowing before you click buy: the reference guide's field testing found white actually reflects more and measures cooler than silver, despite silver's reputation. They cost the same. Either way it matters less than people think — the tarp is 10 ft over your head, so even if it heats up you are fine. If you want colour instead of boring, that is a real option; go light, not dark.

How to tell a good tarp from a bad one

The first thing to fail on playa is always a grommet ripping out, usually a corner. Buy for that:

  • Folded, sewn hem with a rope reinforcement woven through it — this is the one that matters. When a grommet does tear, the rope keeps the whole edge from unzipping.
  • Reinforced corner patches.
  • 12 mil thickness, ~6 oz per square yard, UV-treated and mildew-resistant.
  • Grommets roughly every 18". Heat-sealed seams.

Ball bungees — 6" standard, 9" as backup

Tarps attach to poles with ball bungees. 6" is the standard for this build — one length for every edge on the roof, so there is nothing to sort, nothing to match, and nothing to think about at 2 a.m. The 9" is a backup, not a co-equal partner: it exists for the handful of edges where the reach runs long, and you won't know which ones those are until the frame is standing.

Short answer: 8 bungees per side. One at each corner and six between them, ~16" apart. That is the number to bag by, the number to hand to someone who asks, and the number to count an edge against before you call it done. Per whole tarp it is 28, not 32 — the four corner grommets are each shared between two edges.

Where that number comes from

The count is driven entirely by grommet count, because of the rule below — every grommet gets a bungee. So it is three multiplications, not a guess:

Rule — one length per edge

Every bungee along a single tarp edge must be the same length. Put one 9" in a line of 6" and it sits slack, falls out, and becomes MOOP. Then its neighbours take the extra load, slacken in turn, and the edge unzips itself. So if an edge needs the 9", the whole edge goes to 9" — all eight of them. Never mix along a single run. And every grommet gets a bungee: skipping every other one concentrates the entire wind load on the ones you did use, and they tear out in sequence.

Every panel gets its own bungees. Where two tarps meet on the same pole, that is two separate bungees through two separate grommets — never one bungee through both. Sharing couples the panels: one failure drops two, and you cannot swap a torn tarp without disturbing its neighbour, which throws away the entire reason for going to 10 × 10s.

Why carry a second length at all

Of the 48 tarp edges on this roof, only 14 land on the outside perimeter — that's the 140 ft of it, in 14 ten-foot runs. The other 34 meet back-to-back on an interior pole, in 17 shared pairs. Those shared poles are where the 6" gets tight: the pole already carries another tarp's bungees, two hems stack against each other, and if that bay came out slightly out of square the reach runs long. That is what the 9" is for — one pack, held in reserve, deployed a whole edge at a time.

4 · East wing Already owned — 14 × 20 silver aluminet

The east-facing wing is the 14' × 20' silver aluminet panel purchased last year. Nothing to order, nothing to quote, no lead time — it is in the pile. That removes what used to be the single most schedule-critical line on this sheet.

Aluminet is the right material for this by the guide's own rule. Side panels must be mesh, and knitted aluminet is mesh — wind passes through instead of loading up the frame. Never hang a solid poly tarp as a wall.

The 20-ft dimension is exactly two bays, so the top edge hangs off two of the perimeter roof poles with no improvising. The 14 ft is the slope length running down and out to the ground.

Mounting

  • Slope it, always. Never hang a side panel vertically. A slope gives the wind a chance to go over the top; a vertical face gives it something to push against.
  • Pull it drum-tight. Position the ground anchors so the panel is genuinely taut. Taut, the wind glides up and over. Even slightly slack, it bellies into a sail shape and starts putting real load into the frame.
  • A lag screw + climbing hanger outside the landing point of every grommet along the bottom edge — about 14 anchors across the 20-ft edge at 18" spacing. Not every other one. Every one.
  • Every grommet gets a bungee, top edge and bottom. Half-connecting concentrates the whole wind load on a handful of grommets, which rip in sequence until the panel is gone.
  • The wing is why the diagonal ratchet straps in §2 are not optional.
One thing to confirm: which axis of the frame faces east. If the 40-ft side faces east the 20-ft wing covers half of it; if the 30-ft side faces east it covers two of three bays. Both work — but it determines which two perimeter poles the top edge hangs from, and it should be settled before the frame is squared and drilled down, not after.

7 · Build sequence Decided — tarps go on LAST

Rule — the one that actually gets people hurt

Do not attach a single tarp until the entire frame is up, aligned, drilled down, and fully ratcheted. A tarp on an unanchored frame is a sail on a tumbleweed. If even one vertical strap is missing when a gust hits, that leg lifts and every pole attached to it bends. Bare steel poles catch almost no wind — you can build the whole frame in a blow. You cannot handle tarps in one.

Lay out

Get all 54 poles, 20 footers and 20 five-ways on the ground, roughly where they'll be installed. Decide the 5-way open-axis orientation now (§1) and rotate all 20 to match before anything goes together.

Build cube by cube

Assemble one wall flat on the ground — two legs, one roof pole, two 5-ways. Prop it up by inserting two more legs. Add the remaining roof poles. One person on a step ladder at each free corner holds it while the last verticals go in. Then build the next wall on the ground beside it and join. Eyebolts are hand-tight only here — they're just holding position.

Align — before anything is anchored

Stand at the end of a line of poles and close one eye. If the row is true, every pole behind the front one disappears. Have someone nudge them while you sight. Do this for every row and every column, and check plumb. Loosen and re-tighten eyebolts as needed. This is the last moment it's free to fix.

Drill down

One 3/8" × 14" lag screw through each footer, with a climbing hanger under the head, into the playa. 9/16" 6-point deep impact socket. One screw per footer — the other holes do nothing. 20 legs now; the wing's ~14 ground anchors come later.

Ratchet — vertical, then diagonal

A vertical strap at every single leg: over the 5-way, twisted once or twice around the pole, S-hook into the climbing hanger, tightened hard. Then the 8 internal diagonals. Only when every strap is on and tight is the structure finished. Nothing goes on the roof before this step is complete.

Roof tarps

One 10×10 per bay. Bungee the four corners first, then work every remaining grommet — 8 per edge, 28 per panel. One bungee length per edge, 6" unless that edge won't reach (§3). Twelve panels, all identical, all interchangeable.

East wing

Hang the top edge off the two perimeter poles on the east face, mark where each bottom grommet lands, drill a lag screw + climbing hanger just outside each mark, and bungee it down drum-tight. Sloped, never vertical.

Flag, light, and re-check

Flag and light every hanger and strap run. Then re-tighten every eyebolt, strap and lag screw daily — things work loose in the heat cycle and the vibration.

Crew and time: 5–6 people, two to four step ladders. The cube-by-cube method means you're never lifting the whole 1,200 sq ft assembly at once — which is the other reason it beats build-flat-and-walk-it-up. Budget most of a day and start early enough that you're ratcheting in daylight.

Salty-burner tip worth adopting even with zero cuts: paint or tape one end of a pole batch a unique colour before you leave home. It won't distinguish lengths — everything is 10 ft — but it makes it obvious at a glance which poles are yours when three camps' steel ends up in the same pile.

8 · Field manual Read before you go, not during

It's windy

Above ~50 mph you will not be able to manipulate, hang, fix, or stretch a tarp. It isn't a question of trying harder — it's not possible. Do exactly one thing: pull the bungees and take down any tarp that's threatening the frame. Then get into an RV, truck, or shipping container and wait it out. The heroics you'll see other camps performing accomplish almost nothing and get people hurt.

It's raining

Solid tarps pond. A full bay of standing water pulls down hard enough to threaten the structure — this took camps out in 2023 and 2025. Fix: pull two bungees from one edge of the affected panel to open a spout, then push the centre up gently with the broom to drain it. Twelve small panels pond far less dangerously than one 1,200 sq ft sheet — that's a real safety gain from the 10×10 decision.

Thunder

This is a 1,200 sq ft grounded steel grid. If you see lightning or hear thunder, get yourself and anyone nearby away from it and into a vehicle, RV, truck, or container. Not under the shade — away from it.

Daily walk-around

Re-tighten every ratchet strap, every eyebolt, and every lag screw every day. Heat cycling and constant vibration back everything off. Two minutes a pole, once a day, is the whole maintenance programme.

Don't slope the roof

It's tempting and it's wrong. The flat top is load-bearing design, not laziness. Playa wind is horizontal; a flat roof lets it pass. Give it any pitch and you've built a wing — it lifts the whole structure and flings it.

The twang

A strap running straight from the 5-way to the hanger vibrates and sings in wind, loudly, all night, right over where people sleep. Twist it once or twice around the leg on the way down. Once or twice — more and you can't tension it properly.
